We extend the results of Table 2 of the paper by Buhler, Golan, Pratt, and Wagon (Math. Comp. 90, 2021) aka BGPW.

Background: \(m^*(n)\) is the largest number \(m\) such that \(\{1, \ldots, n\}\) can be split into two parts whose power sums are equal for every exponent \(k = 0, \ldots, m - 1\).

For example, if \(\{1, 2, 3, 4\}\) is partitioned into \(\{1, 4\}\) and \(\{2, 3\}\) then we have these power sums:

  \(k=0\):   \(1^0 + 4^0~=~2^0 + 3^0~=~2\)
  \(k=1\):   \(1^1 + 4^1~=~2^1 + 3^1~=~5\)
  \(k=2\):   \(1^2 + 4^2~= 17~\ne~2^2 + 3^2~=~13\).

Since the power sums are equal for \(k = 0\) and \(k = 1\) but not for \(k = 2\), and since (exercise for the reader) no other bipartition of \(\{1, 2, 3, 4\}\) hits a bigger max \(k\), we say that \(m^*(4) = 2\) or that 4 has order 2.

For \(\{1, \ldots, 8\}\) it turns out there's a bipartition — \(\{1, 4, 6, 7\}\) and \(\{2, 3, 5, 8\}\) — that gets us up to \(k = 2\), meaning \(m^*(8) = 3\):

  \(k=0\):   \(1^0 + 4^0 + 6^0 + 7^0~=~2^0 + 3^0 + 5^0 + 8^0~=~4\)
  \(k=1\):   \(1^1 + 4^1 + 6^1 + 7^1~=~2^1 + 3^1 + 5^1 + 8^1~=~18\)
  \(k=2\):   \(1^2 + 4^2 + 6^2 + 7^2~=~2^2 + 3^2 + 5^2 + 8^2~=~102\)
  \(k=3\):   \(1^3 + 4^3 + 6^3 + 7^3~=~624~\ne~2^3 + 3^3 + 5^3 + 8^3~=~672\).

Notice how the sum of the zeroth powers is just the number of elements. From that we see immediately that every odd \(n\) has order 0 because with an odd number of elements a set can't be partitioned into equal-size subsets, so \(k=0\) already fails.

Fun fact, this property of having equal power sums for exponents from 0 up to some \(k\) is invariant under translating and scaling. In particular that means it doesn't matter if we use \(\{1, \ldots, n\}\) or \(\{0, \ldots, n - 1\}\) as the set to be partitioned. The \(m^*(n)\) values are the same. You can in fact start with any set of \(n\) equally spaced integers.

From here on we use the convention that the \(n\)-element set to be partitioned is \(\{0, 1, \ldots, n - 1\}\).

Bounds on \(m^*(n)\) for every \(n\) up to 1024

The following chart shows \(m^*(n)\) for every \(n\) up to 1024, with the following exceptions:

  • Odd \(n\) are left out; for odd \(n\), \(m^*(n) = 0\)
  • Odd multiples of 2 except 2 itself (6, 10, 14, ...) are left out; for all such \(n\), \(m^*(n) = 1\)

See the introduction for why odd numbers have order 0. A short proof for why odd multiples of 2 have order 1 is as follows. For \(n\) to be an odd multiple of 2 means it's even but not divisible by 4, so twice an odd number. I.e., \(n = 2(2k+1)=4k+2\) for some \(k \ge 0\). As usual, we take our set of \(n\) numbers to be \(\{0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}\). In this case that's \(\{0, 1, \ldots, 4k+1\}\). The sum of that set, by the standard formula for the sum of consecutive numbers, is \((4k+1)(4k+2)/2 = (4k+1)(2k+1)\). That's an odd times an odd, which is odd. So there's no bipartition that splits the sum in half, which means there's no equipowerful partition for exponent 1.

      Asymptotics

      Eyeballing the charts it looks like maybe \(m^*(n) \approx 1.2\log_2 n\). What can we say about the asymptotics? For powers of two we already have this: \(m^*(2^k)/k \to c\) where \(c\) is at most \(2/\log_2 3\), and \(2^{58}\) gives us \(66/58 \approx 1.138\) as the lower bound.

      Relatedly, the upper envelope of \(m^*(n)\) is at least \(\frac{12}{\log_2 480}\log_2 n \approx 1.347\log_2 n\) which we get from Theorem 4.1 (Multiplication) of BGPW which tells us that \(m^*(n^k) \ge k\,m^*(n)\). Namely, \(m^*(480^k) \ge 12k\) so for certain \(n\), \(m^*(n) \ge 12\log_{480} n = 12\log_2 n/\log_2 480 = \frac{12}{\log_2 480}\log_2 n\). So whenever we find a new high watermark for \(m^*(n)/\log_2 n\) that bumps up the upper envelope for the \(m^*\) function. Currently the best we have is 1440:16 (\(\href{#1440:16}{p_{1440}}\)). So \(16/\log_2 1440 \approx 1.525\) is the newest bound on the upper envelope.

      More from Claude:

      The limit \(c\) exists: Multiplication gives \(m^*(2^{a+b}) \ge m^*(2^a) + m^*(2^b)\), so by Fekete's lemma \(m^*(2^k)/k\) converges to its supremum. Its upper end is Boyd's bound (1997, Theorem 1), which the norm bound below sharpens: if a prime \(p\) does not divide \(n\), then \(m^*(n) \le \frac{p-1}{\log_2 p}\log_2 n\). At \(p = 3\) that is \(2/\log_2 3 \approx 1.262\). So only a multiple of 3, as \(1440 = 2^5 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5\) is, can beat that ratio, and only a multiple of 15 can beat \(4/\log_2 5 \approx 1.723\).

      \(m^*(n)/\log_2 n\) has no limit: \(m^*(n)\) is 1 for every \(n \equiv 2 \pmod 4\) and at most 3 for every \(n \equiv 4 \pmod 8\) (BGPW's Theorem 4.1, Divisibility). Its limit superior is the upper envelope's ratio, \(U\), which Multiplication makes equal to \(\sup_n m^*(n)/\log_2 n\), so \(U \ge 1.525\).

      Claude conjectures that \(c = 2/\log_2 3\), the most the norm bound allows. For every \(k\) from 2 to 12, the upper bound from residue sums mod 3, 9 or 27 is attained at \(2^k\), as it is at 39 of the 43 multiples of 16 up to 1024 that are not multiples of 3. That pattern's first test is \(2^{13}\), where \(14 \le m^*(2^{13}) \le 15\), the 15 from the norm bound. The lower end, \(66/58\), is no evidence against the conjecture: at \(2^k\), a join of products of Thue–Morse sequences and blocks up to 1024 can show at most \(\frac{11}{\log_2 800}k \approx 1.141k\). Since \(2^k\) is not a multiple of 3, one of the join's parts is not, nor is any of that part's factors. By the chart's upper bounds, each such factor's order is at most \(\frac{11}{\log_2 800}\) times \(\log_2\) of its length, with equality at 800 among the blocks, and Thue–Morse's is exactly that logarithm.

      Past 1024, 1456 at order 12 would beat 800's ratio: \(12/\log_2 1456 \approx 1.142\). No sequence of length 1456 has order 12. Order 12 there makes every sign \(f_i\) with \(i \equiv 0 \pmod 3\) the same, since 729 must divide the differences of the residue sums mod 3. And \(P(i) = \sum_{k=0}^{11} \psi_k \binom{i}{k}\), with \(\psi = (659,\allowbreak 70,\allowbreak 659,\allowbreak 333,\allowbreak 336,\allowbreak 513,\allowbreak 216,\allowbreak 342,\allowbreak 522,\allowbreak 486,\allowbreak 81,\allowbreak 0)\), is a multiple of 729 at every other \(i < 1456\), while its values at the \(i \equiv 0 \pmod 3\) sum to 243 mod 729. Order 12 makes \(\sum_i f_i P(i) = 0\), so that sum would have to be a multiple of 729 (proof1456.py checks all of this). So \(m^*(1456) = 11\), the 11 from the join of \(\href{#432:11}{p_{432}}\) and \(\href{#1024:11}{p_{1024}}\).

      Whether \(U\) is finite is open. It is finite exactly when the least length of order \(m\) is at least \(a^m\) for some \(a > 1\), and how that length grows is BGPW's last question (§7). They place it between \(e^{(1+o(1))\sqrt m}\) and \(O(192^{m/9})\); 1440:16 lowers the latter to \(O(1440^{m/16})\), from about \(1.793^m\) to about \(1.575^m\). Boyd's bound \(m^*(n) \le (1+o(1))(\ln n)^2/\ln\ln n\) (1997, Remark 1) allows either.

      Where the new results come from

      Claude: “How the upper bounds are proven. Write a partition of \(0, 1, \ldots, n - 1\) as signs \(f_0, \ldots, f_{n-1}\), \(+1\) on one half and \(-1\) on the other, and let \(f(x) = f_0 + f_1x + \cdots + f_{n-1}x^{n-1}\). Order \(m\) means that \((x - 1)^m\) divides \(f(x)\). For a modulus \(M\), the residue sum \(D_r\) is the number of \(+1\)s minus the number of \(-1\)s at the positions \(i \equiv r \pmod{M}\). Reducing \(f(x)\) modulo \(x^M - 1\) leaves \(D_0 + D_1x + \cdots + D_{M-1}x^{M-1}\), which must still be a multiple of \((x - 1)^m\) modulo \(x^M - 1\). Each \(D_r\) also has the parity of its class's size and is at most that size in absolute value. The residue condition mod \(M\) rules out order \(m\) when an exhaustive search finds no residue sums that meet all of this.”

      Claude: “The norm bound for a prime \(p\) that does not divide \(n\) uses the same sums with \(M = p\). Let \(\zeta\) be a primitive \(p\)th root of unity. Then \(f(\zeta) = D_0 + D_1\zeta + \cdots + D_{p-1}\zeta^{p-1}\) is not 0: if it were, the \(D_r\) would all be equal, hence all 0, as they sum to \(f(1) = 0\); but since \(p\) does not divide \(n\), some class has an odd size and so an odd \(D_r\). It is a multiple of \((\zeta - 1)^m\), whose norm is \(p^m\), so the norm of \(f(\zeta)\), a positive integer, is at least \(p^m\). By the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means, that norm is also at most \((p \sum D_r^2 / (p - 1))^{(p-1)/2}\). With \(s_r\) the size of class \(r\) and \(|D_r| \le s_r\), order \(m\) needs \(p^{2m} (p - 1)^{p-1} \le (p \sum s_r^2)^{p-1}\).”

      How Claude found its examples

      The examples Claude found by searching, in the remarks above (\(\href{#320:9}{p_{320}}\), \(\href{#336:9}{q_{336}}\), \(\href{#432:11}{p_{432}}\), \(\href{#560:10}{p_{560}}\) and \(\href{#800:11}{p_{800}}\)) and in early runs at 144 and 240, came from one method: a search for the shortest vectors of a lattice, run with the C++ library fplll.

      Write a sequence as signs \(f_0, \ldots, f_{n-1}\) and let \(x_i = 2i - n + 1\), the positions centered on 0 and doubled. Order \(m\) means \(\sum f_i x_i^k = 0\) for \(k = 0, \ldots, m - 1\): by the fun fact above, these positions give the same order as \(0, \ldots, n - 1\), with smaller numbers. These are \(m\) linear equations in the signs. Claude looked only at symmetric sequences \((f_{n-1-i} = f_i)\) for even \(m\) and antisymmetric ones \((f_{n-1-i} = -f_i)\) for odd \(m\), BGPW's \((-1)^m\) convention. Since \(x_{n-1-i} = -x_i\), that leaves \(h = n/2\) unknown signs \(a_i = f_i\) \((i < h)\) and \(\lfloor m/2\rfloor\) equations, those with \(k\) of the same parity as \(m\); the others hold for every such sequence.

      Put \(a = 2y - 1\). The integer vectors \(y\) for which \(a\) solves the equations form a translate \(y_0 + K\) of the lattice \(K\) of integer solutions of the equations themselves, whose dimension is \(d = h - \lfloor m/2\rfloor\). (If there is no such \(y\), there is no such sequence.) So \(a\) ranges over the translate \(t + 2K\), where \(t = 2y_0 - 1\). Every vector in it has odd entries, so its squared length is at least \(h\), with equality exactly when every entry is \(\pm 1\); any other vector in it has an entry of absolute value at least 3, so its squared length is at least \(h + 8\). So the sequences sought are exactly the shortest vectors of \(t + 2K\), and finding them is a closest-vector problem: find the vectors of the lattice \(2K\) at distance \(\sqrt{h}\) from \(-t\).

      To search it, Claude reduced a basis \(b_1, \ldots, b_d\) of \(2K\) (LLL, then BKZ with block size 20) and enumerated. With \(b_1^*, \ldots, b_d^*\) its Gram–Schmidt vectors, the squared length of \(t + c_1b_1 + \cdots + c_db_d\) is a sum of \(d\) terms, one along each \(b_i^*\), and the term along \(b_i^*\) depends only on \(c_i, \ldots, c_d\). So the enumeration chooses \(c_d\), then \(c_{d-1}\), and so on, trying the integers at each step in order of the term they add (Schnorr–Euchner), and backs up as soon as the sum so far passes \(h\). That tree holds every solution: run to the end, it is an exhaustive search over the sequences of that symmetry, and when it ends empty it proves there are none.

      The tree grows about exponentially with \(d\), so Claude pruned it: once \(j\) of the \(d\) coefficients are chosen, the sum so far must be at most a fraction \(p_j\) of \(h\), with the fractions rising to \(p_d = 1\) (extreme pruning: Gama, Nguyen and Regev, Eurocrypt 2010). A pruned run visits a small part of the tree and finds a given solution with some probability. Randomizing the basis and reducing it again gives another, nearly independent try. In the early runs fplll's Pruner chose the fractions. Every example found was then checked with exact integer power sums.

      lattice_search.wl is a plain Mathematica version of the direct search, without the matching or the fixed residue sums, to time against other search code. In place of fplll it uses LatticeReduce, which does LLL but not BKZ, and its own compiled enumeration, pruned by a simpler rule: the sum so far may be at most \(\min(1, \delta + j/d)\) times \(h\), with \(\delta\) the option "Pruning". \(\delta = 1\), the default, prunes nothing. \(\delta = 0\) is the linear pruning of Gama, Nguyen and Regev, too tight here: the solutions' sums run above \(j/d\) times \(h\), most of all over the first coefficients chosen. An empty list of sequences proves there are none only when the result's "complete" is True, which takes an unpruned search that ran to its end; a search cut off by "MaxNodes" returns False, and its empty list shows only that none turned up in the part of the tree it searched. These calls have known results, in which each sequence's negation counts too:

      • latticeSearch[6, 2, 0]: complete, no sequences: the equations have no integer solution.
      • latticeSearch[32, 5, 0, "Want" -> Infinity]: complete, 2 sequences: Thue–Morse and its negation.
      • latticeSearch[48, 6, 1, "Want" -> Infinity]: complete, 2 sequences: BGPW's \(\href{#48:6}{p_{48}}\) and its negation, as a brute-force check of all \(2^{24}\) symmetric sequences confirms.
      • latticeSearch[64, 6, 0, "Want" -> Infinity]: complete, 6 sequences: Boyd's 3 partitions.
      • latticeSearch[112, 7, -1, "Want" -> Infinity, "MaxNodes" -> 10^9]: complete, 2 sequences: BGPW's \(\href{#112:7}{p_{112}}\) and its negation, after 285 million nodes, about 6 minutes on Danny Reeves's Mac.       • latticeSearch[320, 11, -1, "Want" -> Infinity]: complete, no sequences, after a few nodes: the equations have integer solutions, but none with entries \(\pm 1\).
      • latticeSearch[96, 7, -1]: 1 sequence.
      • latticeSearch[144, 8, 1, "Pruning" -> 0.1]: 1 sequence.

      The examples of 2026-11-03, at 544, 576, 624, 912, 960 and 1008, came from a development of this search, searches/pinned, which keeps the lattice and the enumeration and changes four things.

      • More pinned residue sums. Mod 8, 9, 11, 13, 17 and 19 as well as 5 and 7, and for the central block below also mod 23 and 29. Each prime \(p\) removes \((p - 1)/2\) dimensions, and up to about \(p = 29\) a Gaussian estimate of the number of solutions per coset does not fall, because a sequence's residue sums at small \(p\) are nearly forced anyway. At 1008, for order 13, that estimate went from about \(2^{-6}\) at \(d = 153\) to about \(2^{20}\) at \(d = 125\).
      • Residue sums from the right coset. Mod 2 every sequence of length \(n\) is the same polynomial, so the residue sums mod \(M\) lie in one coset of \(2L(M, m)\). At \(n = 16\) times an odd number and high order that forces residue sums mod 4 that are not 0.
      • The fixed signs. They are annealed on the squared length of \(t\)'s part outside the span of \(2K\), each unit of which costs 0.72 bits of expected solutions. Congruences that the free set imposes on them are met first by class sums mod 81, then by a meet in the middle over swaps that keep those sums.
      • The free set. The positions divisible by 3 when 3 divides \(n\); the others when it does not, the class of 0 mod 3 being nearly fixed then; or a central block, which imposes no congruences, the fixed signs annealed ring by ring from the outside in, each ring measured against what the positions inside it can still reach.

      The times of the runs that found them: 1008 in about 1 CPU minute per seed, 912 in 45 seconds, 624 in 97 seconds and 544 in under 2 minutes; 576 in about 11 minutes on 5 cores; and 960, with the central block, in about 100 minutes in all on 6 to 12 cores. The README in that directory has the commands.

      The examples of 2026-11-04, of order 13 at 672 and of order 16 at 1440, came from a further development, searches/tilted, which keeps the pinned residue sums, the lattice and the enumeration and changes three things.

      • Each sign's bias. Pinned residue sums can leave some signs nearly decided. Maximizing entropy subject to the pins gives each sign an expected value, and from those an estimate of the number of solutions that no longer assumes the signs are balanced.
      • The fixed signs. A beam search chooses them, favoring the signs' biases and the squared length of \(t\)'s part outside the span of \(2K\). Each run adds its own random field to the biases, so that runs reach different cosets.
      • A sagging bound. The enumeration's bound runs below the straight line through the middle of the tree. At 1008, for order 13, in 180 seconds on one core: 43 examples with no sag, 989 with a sag of 0.45, and 1 with a sag of 0.6.

      The runs that found them took 532 seconds at 672 and 709 seconds at 1440, each on one core. The README in that directory has the commands.

      How Claude ruled out order 12 at 432 and order 13 at 816

      Finding an example needs one sequence, and can look only at symmetric ones. Ruling an order out must cover every sequence, about \(2^n\) of them. These proofs do it with a tree, a pin tree: each node pins some residue sums \(D_r\) to given values, standing for the sequences with those sums; the root pins nothing; and every leaf is shown impossible. Three things make the tree small.

      The parity coset. The residue condition says that the residue sums mod \(M\), as a polynomial mod \(x^M - 1\), are a multiple of \((x - 1)^m\): they lie in a lattice \(L(M, m)\), with each \(D_r\) of the parity of its class's size. But mod 2 every sign is 1, so every sequence of length \(n\) is the same polynomial, \(S = (x^n - 1)/(x - 1)\). Dividing \(f\) by \((x - 1)^m\) therefore gives one fixed quotient mod 2, and so, mod a power of 2, the residue sums lie in a single coset of \(2L(M, m)\), which is far smaller. For order 12 at 432, mod 8, that leaves 5,450 vectors of 186,309. It holds for every sequence, symmetric or not.

      Certificates. Take a polynomial \(g\) of degree below \(m\) and a weight \(c_M(r)\) for each class of each pinned modulus, and let \(w(i) = g(i) + \sum_M c_M(i \bmod M)\). For a sequence of order \(m\) with those pins, \(\sum_i w(i) f_i\) is a number \(K\) known from the pins alone, \(\sum_M \sum_r c_M(r) D^{(M)}_r\), because \(\sum_i g(i) f_i = 0\). But \(|\sum_i w(i) f_i| \le \sum_i |w(i)|\). So if \(|K| > \sum_i |w(i)|\), no sequence has those pins. A linear program over \(-1 \le f_i \le 1\) finds such weights whenever the pins are impossible even for real numbers.

      Splitting. A node that no certificate closes splits on a further modulus. The same linear program bounds each residue sum of that modulus, and each bound is a certificate too; every vector of the lattice inside those bounds becomes a child. The modulus chosen is the one with fewest vectors, mostly 9, 11, 13 or 27. The tree's top pins 3, 5, 7, 4 and 8.

      It works here because 5 and 7 divide neither length, so the residue sums mod 5 and 7 cannot vanish, and the residue condition then forces them to be large. At 432, for order 12, the sums mod 5 must be \(\pm(75, 75, -50, -50, -50)\), on classes of 87, 87, 86, 86 and 86: two classes have only 6 signs out of 87 against the rest. A few such periodic patterns soon ask more of the signs than any can give, and nodes close quickly.

      • 432, order 12: 3,119 certificates, closing 7,561 nodes after 101 splits. About 15 CPU-minutes to make, 7 seconds to check.
      • 816, order 13: 187,269 certificates, closing 332,497 nodes after 6,269 splits. About 21 CPU-hours to make, 4 minutes to check.

      The linear program runs in floating point and only suggests. pintree.py replays a proof in exact integers with nothing but Python's standard library, and its docstring states the whole argument. pintree_second.py checks the same file with no code in common, enumerating and evaluating by other routes. Both accept the proof at 432 and the proof at 816, with the same counts. Each known sequence tried, asymmetric ones among them, survives the whole procedure at its own order.

      Other open cells have nodes where the residue sums are small and the linear program stays loose. Estimates for those run from hundreds of CPU-days to far more, so this method does not reach them yet. searches/pintree has the code that makes the proofs, the estimates and more of the details.

      BGPW's Symmetry Conjecture through order 11

      BGPW conjecture (their §6) that if some sequence of length \(n\) has order \(m\), then some \((-1)^m\)-symmetric one does, with \(f_{n-1-i} = (-1)^m f_i\): symmetric for even \(m\), antisymmetric for odd \(m\). They proved it for \(m \le 8\) (their Theorem 3.2). Claude has shown that it holds for every \(m \le 11\) and every \(n\), given the upper bounds listed below. A second Claude, writing its own code to look for an error, confirmed this and corrected that list.

      Where that leaves the conjecture:

      • Orders up to 8: proved by BGPW (their Theorem 3.2), from the upper bounds at 96, 152, 168, 176 and 184 listed below. Berend and Golan proved those at 96 and 152 by hand, and since 2026-11-09 form-tree proofs here check all five, those at 168, 176 and 184 for the first time.
      • Orders 9, 10 and 11: proved here at every length, from upper bounds that each have a checked proof. Two of those proofs came on 2026-11-09, by form trees: at 272, where the bound had come from BGPW's integer linear programs, which, they write, cannot be certified, and at 752, where it had come from a computation with no certificate. The bound at 144, which Berend and Golan proved by hand, got a second proof by form tree the same day.
      • Order 10 at 288: settled on 2026-11-08. The form-tree proof shows that no sequence of length 288 has order 10, so orders 9 to 11 are done, given those bounds.
      • Orders 12 and up: open. Orders 12, 13 and 16 hold at every sufficiently large length, and orders 14, 15 and 17 and up would each follow at large lengths from one more example (the last paragraphs below). At every order, finitely many small lengths remain, each needing its own computation.

      Three facts reduce it to finitely many lengths.

      • Divisibility. If \(2^v\) is the largest power of 2 that divides \(n\), no sequence of length \(n\) has order \(2^v\) (BGPW's Theorem 4.1). So orders 9 to 11 need \(16 \mid n\).
      • Symmetry. The exact order of a symmetric sequence is even, and that of an antisymmetric one odd (BGPW's Theorem 4.1). So let \(s = (-1)^m\), let \(p\) have order \(m - 1\), whatever its symmetry, and let \(q\) be empty or \(s\)-symmetric of order \(m\). Then \(p \vee q \vee s p^*\), with \(p^*\) the reversal of \(p\), is \(s\)-symmetric of order \(m - 1\), and so of order \(m\).
      • A tail. Suppose every multiple of 16 from \(A\) to \(2A - 16\) has a sequence of order \(m - 1\). Then joins give one at every multiple of 16 from \(A\) on. With those as \(p\), an empty \(q\) gives an \((-1)^m\)-symmetric sequence of order \(m\) at every multiple of 32 from \(2A\) on, and a \(q\) of length \(b\), an odd multiple of 16, gives one at every odd multiple of 16 from \(2A + b\) on.

      For orders 9 to 11 these values work. The chart of \(m^*(n)\) above shows the orders needed from \(A\) to \(2A - 16\), but for 1040, a join of 240 and 800.

      • \(m = 9\): \(A = 192\), and \(b = 240\) for BGPW's antisymmetric example of order 9 (BGPW's Table 5), so the conjecture holds for every \(n \ge 624\).
      • \(m = 10\): \(A = 320\), and \(b = 240\) for BGPW's symmetric example of order 10, so it holds for every \(n \ge 880\).
      • \(m = 11\): \(A = 528\), and \(b = 432\) for \(\href{#432:11}{p_{432}}\), so it holds for every \(n \ge 1488\).

      Every large length. One more statement holds past some length at every order, given a theorem of Freiman and Litsyn (1999) that BGPW quote in their §3, which Claude has not read: for each order \(k\), every sufficiently large length that Divisibility allows has a sequence of order \(k\). Fix \(m\), let \(s = (-1)^m\), and let \(2^a\) be the least power of 2 above \(m\), so that every length of order \(m\) is a multiple of \(2^a\). Then the conjecture holds at every sufficiently large length if and only if some \(s\)-symmetric sequence of order \(m\) has a length \(b\) that is \(2^a\) times an odd number.

      At a length \(n\) divisible by \(2^{a+1}\), take \(p\) of order \(m - 1\) and length \(n/2\): \(p \vee s p^*\) is \(s\)-symmetric of order \(m - 1\), and so, by Symmetry, of order \(m\). At a length \(n\) that is \(2^a\) times an odd number, \(n - b\) is divisible by \(2^{a+1}\), and \(p \vee q \vee s p^*\), with \(q\) that sequence of length \(b\) and \(p\) of order \(m - 1\) and length \((n - b)/2\), is \(s\)-symmetric of order \(m\) in the same way. Conversely, the theorem gives sequences of order \(m\) at every large length that is \(2^a\) times an odd number, so there the conjecture needs such a \(q\).

      The page's codes are all \((-1)^m\)-symmetric, and three of them are such sequences: 912:12 for order 12 (\(912 = 16 \cdot 57\)), 1008:13 for order 13 (\(1008 = 16 \cdot 63\)) and 1440:16 for order 16 (\(1440 = 32 \cdot 45\)). Orders 14 and 15 would follow from one such sequence each, at a length 16 times an odd number, and none is known: the explicit sequences of order 14 or more, and their joins and products, all have lengths that are multiples of 32. Below those lengths, each order leaves finitely many lengths, and where no symmetric sequence exists, as BGPW found at 288 for order 10, the conjecture there says that no sequence of that order exists at all.
